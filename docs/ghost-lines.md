# Ghost lines

English lines that were good enough to keep, and that Jafari never wrote.

Each one came from a corrupt transcription: a translator (human or machine) read a mangled
Persian token, made honest sense of it, and produced English with nothing behind it. They are
recorded here for two reasons. They should not creep back into the book as if they were
translations — and some of them are worth having anyway, as original English, belonging to
whoever wrote them.

---

## "Tonight I painted the bruise-blue sound of my song."

**Withdrawn from** `preface/04-coda-from-jafari.html`, July 2026.

The handwritten page that closes the book opens `امشب صدای کبوترانم را نقاشی کردم` — *tonight I
painted the voices of my doves.* An OCR pass split `کبوترانم` ("my doves") into two words,
`کبود` ("livid, bruise-blue") and `ترانم` (read as "my song"), and the English followed the
split faithfully. There is no bruise and no blue in the line; there are doves.

The printed twin of that page — page 72 of the book, now in the collection as
`poems/adobe-birdsong.poem` — is what settled it.

The line is nobody's translation, so it is nobody's but the translator's. **Ben's, to use as
his own** if he wants it: it is a better first line than most, and it costs the book nothing to
let it live here instead.

Three others withdrawn from the same page at the same time, and not worth keeping:

- "the spinning sweet breads" — from `گردان نان‌های ناز`, itself an OCR phantom
- "Persian and the green line" — from `پرسی و خط آلی`, which is `و خط‌های آبی‌رنگ`, *the blue
  lines*
- "You were like a brazier" — from `بودی کامنگل`, which is `بوی کاهگل`: *the smell of adobe*,
  the phrase the book is named after

## "out of *I do not know* grows *I know*"

**Withdrawn from** `preface/01-jafari-words.html`, July 2026.

The printed page reads `الهام هرچه باشد از نمی‌دانم دائم می‌روید` — *whatever inspiration may be,
it grows continually out of I do not know* — and the footnote attributes it to Szymborska, whose
Nobel lecture has "whatever inspiration is, it's born from a continuous *I don't know*."

`دائم` ("continually") had been transcribed as `دانم` ("I know"), which turned an open-ended
statement into a closed aphorism: not-knowing *producing* knowing. It survived two translation
passes and a written defence in `claude-comment.md` before a photograph of the page killed it.

Worth keeping as a cautionary specimen rather than as a line: **it was a better aphorism than
the real one, which is exactly why nobody caught it.** The genuine line is humbler and truer —
inspiration keeps growing out of not-knowing, and never arrives at knowing at all.
