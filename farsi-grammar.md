# Farsi Grammar Notes

A running reference for grammatical points that come up while translating Jafari's
poems. One section per point; append new ones as they arise. Transliteration is given
in *(italics, parentheses)* using a loose scheme (â = long "a" as in "father").

---

## The suffix ـانه (-âne) — "in the manner of," "-ly"

**What it does.** `ـانه` (*-âne*) attaches to a noun or adjective and turns it into a
word meaning "in the manner of / in the way characteristic of" that thing. Most often
it forms an **adverb of manner** (English "-ly"), but the same form can act as an
**adjective** ("of the … kind"). It is fully productive — you can coin it on almost any
human-quality noun.

- عاشق (*âsheq*, "lover / one in love") → عاشقانه (*âsheqâne*, "lovingly, amorously, as a lover does")

**Example — `bomb-kiss`, line 2:**

> تا تو را عاشقانه ببوسم
> *tâ to râ âsheqâne bebusam*
> "so that I may kiss you **lovingly**" → rendered "to kiss you **the way lovers do**"

Word by word:

| Persian | translit | role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| تا | *tâ* | "so that, in order to" — purpose conjunction; forces the **subjunctive** verb |
| تو | *to* | "you" (singular) |
| را | *râ* | direct-object marker — flags تو as the definite object being kissed |
| عاشقانه | *âsheqâne* | **adverb of manner**, "lovingly" (عاشق "lover" + ـانه) |
| ببوسم | *bebusam* | "(that) I kiss" — subjunctive of بوسیدن *busidan* "to kiss," 1st-sing. (بـ + stem بوس + ـم) |

**Why it sits where it does.** Persian is Subject–Object–Verb, and a manner adverb
normally drops into the slot **right before the verb**. Here the order is
[*tâ*] [object *to râ*] [manner *âsheqâne*] [verb *bebusam*]. That position, plus the
*-âne* shape, is what makes عاشقانه unambiguously modify "kiss" rather than describe "you."

**Why "the way lovers do" rather than just "lovingly."** English "lovingly" flattens it
to a generic warmth. The literal force of *-âne* is "**in the manner characteristic of
an عاشق** (a lover)" — it points at the *kind of agent* whose behavior is being imitated.
"The way lovers do" keeps that "in the manner of a lover" sense and lets the line land on
the act that the bomb is about to interrupt. It's a slightly freer choice; "lovingly" is
the safe literal.

**Adverb vs. adjective — tell them apart by position:**
- Before a verb → adverb: عاشقانه می‌نویسد (*âsheqâne minevisad*) "he writes lovingly."
- After/with a noun → adjective: نامهٔ عاشقانه (*nâme-ye âsheqâne*) "a love letter,"
  شعر عاشقانه "a love poem."

**Other common -âne words you'll meet in these poems and elsewhere:**

| word | from | meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| دوستانه *dustâne* | دوست "friend" | in a friendly way |
| مردانه *mardâne* | مرد "man" | manfully, manly |
| کودکانه *kudakâne* | کودک "child" | childlike, childish |
| شاعرانه *shâ'erâne* | شاعر "poet" | poetically |
| صادقانه *sâdeqâne* | صادق "honest" | sincerely, honestly |
| عارفانه *ârefâne* | عارف "mystic" | mystically |
| دیوانه‌وار *divâne-vâr* | دیوانه "mad" | like a madman (note: this uses a *different* "like" suffix, ـوار *-vâr*, not *-âne* — same idea, different ending) |

> Note the last row: Persian has more than one "in the manner of" suffix. ـانه (*-âne*)
> and ـوار (*-vâr*) overlap heavily; ـوار leans toward "resembling / -like" and is common
> in poetry (Jafari uses دیوانه‌وار, "madly," repeatedly).

### Is ـانه vs. ـوار a dialect thing? (No.)

Both are **standard literary/Classical Persian** and fully at home in the standard
register — *-âne* descends from Middle Persian *-ânag*, *-vâr* is equally old. The
difference between them is **register and shade of meaning** (*-vâr* is more archaic and
poetic, "resembling"), not dialect. Choosing دیوانه‌وار over a *-âne* form is a stylistic
choice, not a regional one.

Jafari's genuine **dialect / colloquial** moments live in different machinery —
**phonology and verb endings**, not these suffixes. Watch for the spoken-register shifts:

| colloquial (as printed) | standard | what changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| آسمون *âsemun* | آسمان *âsemân* | *ân* → *un* before/near *n* |
| می‌دونی *midoni* / نمی‌دونه | می‌دانی / نمی‌داند | stem *dân-* → *don-* |
| نداره *nadâre* | ندارد *nadârad* | dropped final *-d* of 3rd-sing. |
| بارون *bârun* | باران *bârân* | *ân* → *un* |
| اگه *age* | اگر *agar* | clipped |

Those forms show up in **`sky-swallows`** ("…به طاق آسمونند", "نمی دونی") and
**`nightingale-cloud`** ("بلبل نداره… اگه بارون نباره"), which are written in a folk /
colloquial voice. The *-âne* / *-vâr* suffixes are orthogonal to all of that — you'll see
them in his most standard, literary poems too.

### Translating ـانه into English: the suffix gap (why "manfully," not "manly")

Your instinct is right — English *has* this manner-from-a-noun move, it just doesn't have
**one** productive switch for it the way Persian does. The reason is that English **-ly is
overloaded**; it does two different jobs:

1. **noun → adjective:** man→man**ly**, friend→friend**ly**, love→love**ly**, ghost→ghost**ly**, cost→cost**ly**
2. **adjective → adverb:** quick→quick**ly**, sad→sad**ly**

Because *-ly* is already "used up" making the *adjective* (manly, friendly), English can't
comfortably stack a second one to make the manner adverb — there's no *manlily* or
*friendlily*. So for the **manner-adverb** sense (Persian's *-âne*), English scatters the
job across several frozen devices:

- **-fully:** man→man**ful**→man**fully**, grace→graceful→graceful**ly**, art→artful→artful**ly**, master→masterful→masterful**ly**. This is the closest structural cousin of *-âne*, and it's exactly why **مردانه → "manfully"** feels right: *manful* = "showing the qualities of a man," *manfully* = "in that manner." That is *-âne*'s "in the manner characteristic of."
- **"in a ___ way / manner":** the periphrastic catch-all — "in a friendly way," "the way lovers do" (our عاشقانه rendering, reached for precisely because there's no clean *loverly*-adverb).
- **-wise** (clockwise, likewise), **-fashion / -style** (crab-fashion), **-like** (childlike — overlaps with *-vâr*).

**manly vs. manfully, precisely:**

- **manly** = *adjective*; names the static **nature** of a thing — "a manly voice." Tied to a noun.
- **manfully** = *adverb of manner*; describes **how an action is performed**, usually with a tint of effort/fortitude — "he bore it manfully." Tied to a verb.

Persian **مردانه is one word for both** (position tells you which): before a verb it's the
adverb ("manfully, forthrightly, bravely"); with a noun it's the adjective — لباس مردانه
"men's clothing," حرف مردانه "a straight, honest word." English splits that single form
into two words (*manly* adj / *manfully* adv) — **and only some nouns even get the
*-fully* adverb at all** (manfully, gracefully, artfully, gleefully, scornfully, but no
*\*poetfully*, *\*tablefully*). That frozenness is the asymmetry you're feeling: English
owns the *capacity* but not the *productivity*. Persian *-âne* is a single live switch you
can throw on almost any human-quality noun; the English translator has to pick among
*-fully* / *-ly* / "in a ___ way" / *-wise* / *-like* — or go periphrastic — case by case.

One caution for word choice: "manfully" has drifted toward "**enduring hardship bravely**,"
while مردانه can be neutral ("in a manly / forthright manner") or specifically "bravely"
depending on the line. Pick by context, not reflex.

## افتادن (*oftâdan*) — "to fall," and its "befall / occur" shading

**Two things are easy to conflate here.** The *completed* feel of the simple past افتاد
(*oftâd*) is **aspect**, not the verb's meaning: Persian simple past is perfective by
default, so بمب افتاد already reads "the bomb fell, *done*." That finishedness is the tense,
not a special "it occurred" sense of the verb.

**The "happen / befall" sense is real but mostly lives in collocations** — above all
اتفاق افتادن (*ettefâq oftâdan*), lit. "an event falls" = "to happen" (same logic as English
*be-fall* → *befall*); also راه افتادن "get going," پس افتادن "collapse," جا افتادن "settle in."
But **standalone, with a concrete subject, افتادن snaps back to the literal "fall / drop."**

**Example — `bomb-kiss`, line 3:**

> بمب افتاد...
> *bomb oftâd…*
> "the bomb fell / dropped…"

**Would it carry "the bomb struck"?** Slight overshoot. افتادن encodes the *descent / landing*,
not the *blow* — "struck" foregrounds forceful impact, which the verb doesn't lexicalize. For
real impact Persian reaches for **خوردن (به)** ("hit / land on": بمب به خانه خورد), **اصابت کردن**
(formal "strike a target": بمب اصابت کرد), or **زدن** ("strike," but it wants an agent/object —
بمب زدند "they bombed"; an intransitive bomb-as-subject "struck" is more خوردن/اصابت than زدن).
So "struck" is a defensible interpretive step toward impact, not a literal equivalence.

Also: the trailing ellipsis (افتاد**...**) is the fall and then the held breath — "the bomb
fell…" keeps that hush; "the bomb struck." closes it off hard.

## The root و-ق-ع (*w-q-ʿ*) — "to fall," and why reality and event are the same word

**واقعیت** (*vâqe'iyat*, "reality") and **واقعه** (*vâqe'e*, "event, occurrence") are not
two words that happen to look alike. Both are built on the Arabic triliteral root **و-ق-ع**,
"to fall, to drop, to befall." Reality is *what has fallen out*; an event is *what falls*.
The same root gives **وقوع** (*voqu'*, "occurrence"), **موقع** (*mowqe'*, "the place or time
something falls" → occasion, position), **موقعیت** (situation), **توقع** (*towqo'*,
"expectation" — what one supposes will fall out), and the همه‌جا-collocation
**اتفاق افتادن**, which does the identical thing in Persian with a Persian verb
(see the افتادن entry above: "an event falls" = "to happen").

**Where it bites — Jafari's aphorism in `preface/01-jafari-words.html`:**

> وقتی نگاهی نو به واقعیت می‌کنی، واقعیت به واقعه تبدیل می‌شود.
> *vaqti negâhi now be vâqe'iyat mikoni, vâqe'iyat be vâqe'e tabdil mishavad.*

The aphorism *is* the etymology: look at the fallen-out world freshly and it starts falling
again. "Reality turns into an occurrence" states the sense and drops the pun.

**English has the same figure, in Latin.** *Cadere*, "to fall," gives **case** (what is the
case = what has fallen out), **occasion** (*ob-cadere*, falling toward), **incident**
(*in-cadere*, falling into), **accident**, **chance**, **cadence**, **decay**. So the pun can
be carried across by falling in a different language: **reality → occasion** keeps a
fall on both sides of the sentence, where *reality → event* (*ex-venire*, "to come out")
loses it. That is why the preface reads "reality turns into an occasion."

## سیل کردن (*sil kardan*) in Lori and Bakhtiari — "to look," not "to flood"

In standard Persian **سیل** is *seyl*, "flood, torrent," and a reader who meets
سیل کرد in a line of verse will reach for water. In the **Lori / Bakhtiari / Laki**
dialects of the Zagros, however, **سیل کردن** (*sil kardan*) is the ordinary verb for
**to look, to gaze** — سیلم کن, "look at me" — where standard Persian would use
نگاه کردن. The two are false friends, and dialect quatrains are exactly where the
trap is set, because the *do-bayti* is the Zagros form par excellence (Baba Taher).

**Where it bites — the folk quatrain quoted by Sinai in `preface/02-sinai-pomegranate.html`:**

> سفیده ناله سو... سیل شفق کرد
> "the white one gave a moan… and **looked at** the dawn-glow"

An earlier English had the dawn-glow *flooding* red, which inverts the grammar: شفق is the
**object** of the looking, not the subject of a flood. The dove looks; the dawn is looked at.

Other markers of the same dialect layer in that quatrain, all of them contractions that
drop a standard Persian consonant: **آفتو** ← آفتاب (sun), **مِزه** ← مزه (taste),
**مِثه** ← مثل (like), **زعفرونی** ← زعفرانی (saffron-colored), **دِ** ← در (in),
**سایه‌بون** ← سایه‌بان (awning, arbor), **نشستن** for نشستند (3rd plural past without ـد).

## کوره‌راه (*kure-râh*) — a byway, with no kiln in it

**کوره‌راه** is a single lexical item: a **narrow track, byway, footpath** — the small
unmade road between fields or over a hill. It is *not* کوره (*kure*, "kiln, furnace")
compounded with راه ("road"), however invitingly it decomposes. The first element is the
older *kur-/kura-* "small, blind, dead-end" that also sits in کوره‌ده (a hamlet, a
one-horse village) and کوره‌سواد (a scrap of literacy) — a diminishing, dead-end sense,
not a heat sense. English **byroad**, **back road**, **cart track** all land it.

**Where it bites — `poems/road-peasant.poem`, line 1:**

> در کوره راه ها....
> "on the back roads…"

The machine pass produced "In the furnace of the roads," and the first lantern half-kept the
error as "the rough kiln-hot tracks" — an image of heat that the Persian does not contain,
and one that fights the poem's own palette (blue, gray, black, rain-beaten thorns). Corrected
to "On the back roads," which also matches Farrokhi's quotation of the poem in the preface.

## کم‌رنگ / پررنگ — how Persian turns a colour down and up

**It is not a colour system.** It is Persian's ordinary compounding — کم ("little, low") and پر
("full, much") prefixed to a noun — applied to رنگ, "colour":

- **کم‌رنگ** (*kam-rang*), "low-coloured" → pale, faint, dilute, washed out
- **پررنگ** (*por-rang*), "full-coloured" → deep, saturated, bold

The pattern `<colour> + کم‌رنگ` gives the light shade of that colour: آبی کم‌رنگ light blue,
قرمز کم‌رنگ pale red. It is not a comparative — that would be کم‌رنگ‌تر — so سبز کم‌رنگ is *a pale
green*, not *a greener/less green than something*.

**But "less green" catches something "pale green" loses.** English *pale green* names a tint you
could buy in a tube; Persian says *green, low on colour*. And the same two prefixes run through the
whole language, which is what gives کم‌رنگ its flavour: کم‌نور (dim, "low-light"), کم‌عمق (shallow),
کم‌حرف (taciturn), کم‌رو (shy, "low-face"), **کم‌خون (anaemic, "low-blood")** — against پرنور (bright),
پرکار (industrious), پرحرف (talkative). **کم‌ words are deficiency words.** So کم‌رنگ says the colour
is *insufficient*, in the same breath the language uses for anaemia and dimness — which is why
"faintly green" or "a thin green" can be truer to it than the flat "pale green."

Other axes, not to be confused with this one:

- **تیره / روشن** — dark / light. سبز تیره dark green, سبز روشن light green. This is *value*.
- **سیر** — literally "sated, full-fed", so سبز سیر is a deep, replete green.
- **رنگ‌پریده** — "colour-fled," of a face: pale, pallid, blanched.
- **بی‌رنگ** colourless; **رنگین** many-coloured, and رنگین‌تر "more coloured."

**Where it bites — one dial, two poems, four pages apart:**

> `adobe-birdsong` (p. 72), the title poem —
> بوی کاهگل / آواز پرنده را / **پررنگ‌تر** می‌کند
> "the smell of adobe makes the bird's song *more deeply coloured*"

> `war-delirium` (p. 68) —
> و اشک‌های تو / سبز **کم‌رنگ** بودند
> "and your tears would be *a pale green*"

The book's title sentence turns the saturation **up**; the war poem finds it turned **down**. Same
dial, opposite ends — and پررنگ‌تر returns a third time in the Alizadeh letter (نَفَسِ پرنده... با سازِ
تو... پررنگ‌تر می‌شود). An English that renders one end as "deepens the colour" and the other as
"thins" keeps the two related; reaching for an unrelated word at either end drops a line that runs
through the whole book.

## تاب (*tâb*) — two roots wearing one spelling

**تاب** carries three live senses in Persian — *shine*, *twist*, and *endurance* — and they
do not come from one word. Two separate Iranian roots fell together into an identical form,
and classical poetry has been mining the collision ever since.

**Root 1 — تابیدن (*tâbidan*) "to shine, radiate, burn."** Proto-Iranian \*tap-, from PIE
\*tep- "to be warm." Cognate with Latin *tepēre* (English *tepid*) and Sanskrit *tapati*
"heats, shines" / *tapas* "heat, ascetic ardor." Its Persian family:

- تب (*tab*) "fever"
- تابستان (*tâbestân*) "summer"
- تابش (*tâbesh*) "radiance"; تابان, تابنده "shining"
- مهتاب (*mahtâb*) "moonlight"; آفتاب (*âftâb*) "sunlight"

**Root 2 — تابیدن / تافتن (*tâbidan / tâftan*) "to twist, spin, wind."** A distinct root
whose deeper reconstruction is best held loosely. Its family:

- تاب (*tâb*) "a twist, a curl" — **تابِ زلف** (*tâb-e zolf*), the curl of the beloved's
  lock, a stock ghazal image
- پیچ و تاب (*pich-o-tâb*) "twists and turns," writhing
- تاب (*tâb*) "a swing" — the thing that oscillates
- تاب خوردن "to warp, to buckle"

**The third sense comes off Root 2.** *tâb* as **endurance, tensile strength, the capacity
to bear** is generally derived from the twist root on a rope metaphor: a twisted cord has
strength to hold against a pull. Hence تاب آوردن (*tâb âvardan*) "to endure, bear up," and

- **بی‌تاب** (*bi-tâb*) — "without twist" → without tensile strength → unable to bear it →
  **restless, impatient, languishing**; in the lover's register, *pining*.

**Why the other sense still hums.** Because Root 1 is spelled and pronounced identically,
بی‌تاب also reads at the edge of hearing as **"without shine" — lustreless**. Classical
poetry works this seam deliberately: the beloved's *tâb-e zolf* both twists and gleams, and
the lover is *bi-tâb* both without patience and without light.

**Where it bites — `poems/tree-eyes.poem`:**

> من درختم / زرد خورشید / آبی آب / سبز بی‌تاب
Chafing green was chosen here eventually.
The primary reading of سبز بی‌تاب is Root 2: **restless green**, and that is not in doubt.
But the poem's other two colours are borrowed straight from light-sources — the sun's yellow,
the water's blue — so a green that is *bi-tâb* is also, in overtone, a green with no lamp
behind it. The unrest and the dimness are the same word. Not the meaning; an overtone, and
one a stacked colour-list makes audible in a way ordinary prose would not.

Note also that بی‌تاب sits in the poem's rhyme spine — آب (*âb*) → بی‌تاب (*bi-tâb*) →
ناب (*nâb*) — which argues that the water, the unrest, and the purity are one substance.
No English rendering gets the chain; say so in `===footnotes===` rather than forcing it.

**Not related:** Arabic طاب (*tâba*, "to be good, pleasant"), spelled with ط, is a wholly
separate word that only looks adjacent in transliteration. تب (*tab*) "fever" *is* family.
