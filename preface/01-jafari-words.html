فارسی کوتاه‌ترین غزلی که می‌شناسم «بید مجنون» است. شنیده‌ام: هنر حیاتی شدت یافته است. عشق با نگاه شدّت‌یافته آغاز می‌شود، نگاهی درخشنده و تابناک. اگر شگفتی امروز را تا آخرین قطره بنوشی، فردا تشنه‌تر خواهی بود. یک نفس از امروز را به فردا مگذار، آن که در دیروز نفس می‌کشد، امروز آوازی تازه ندارد. میان سنت و نوگرایی پلی است که با پاهای شوق پیموده می‌شود.
از هم‌آغوشی جانم با شوق
در پی تاب و تبی پیچاپیچ
سایه‌ای می‌روید
در پسِ پردهٔ هیچ...
وقتی نگاهی نو به واقعیت می‌کنی، واقعیت به واقعه تبدیل می‌شود. باغ شعر من در این سایه‌ها گل داده است. من باور دارم که...

محمد ابراهیم جعفری ۱۳۹۶
١ ویساوا شیمبورسکا، شاعر لهستانی، برندهٔ جایزهٔ نوبل ادبیات، (۱۹۲۳–۲۰۱۲).
٢ آرچی بالد مکلیش، شاعر و کتابدار امریکایی، (۱۸۹۲–۱۹۸۲).
English The shortest ghazal I know is the weeping willow.
Translator’s note

بید مجنون (bid-e majnun), the Persian name for the weeping willow, is literally Majnun's willow — named for the madman-lover of Layli. The tree is already a love poem: a name out of a romance, a shape that hangs like a lament. English keeps the weeping and loses the lover.

I have heard it said: art is a life intensified. Love begins with a gaze intensified — a gaze that shines, a gaze that blazes. Drink today's wonder to the last drop and tomorrow you will be thirstier still. Don't leave a single breath of today for tomorrow. Whoever breathes in yesterday has no new song today. Between tradition and the new there stands a bridge, and it is paced out on the feet of ardor.
From my soul's coupling with ardor,
in the wake of a heat and a fever, writhing,
a shadow grows
behind the curtain of nothing…
Translator’s note

روییدن (ruidan) is the verb for a plant coming up, and it is this page's own refrain: a shadow grows here; inspiration grows continually out of I do not know at the foot of the page; and in Farrokhi's introduction a peasant's fortune has grown like a shadow beside his feet. It is given as "grow" in all three. The Persian also rhymes پیچاپیچ (coil upon coil) with هیچ (nothing) — writhing / nothing is a reach for that chime.

When you look at reality with a new eye, reality turns into an occasion.
Translator’s note

واقعیت (vâqe'iyat, reality) and واقعه (vâqe'e, event) are one root, Arabic و-ق-ع, "to fall, to befall": reality is what has fallen out, an event is what falls. English can hold the pun only by falling in Latin — case, occasion, incident, accident all come down from cadere, "to fall." Hence "occasion" rather than "event."

My garden of poetry has come into flower in these shadows. I believe that…
"Whatever inspiration may be, it grows continually out of I do not know."1
I believe that…
"A poem should not mean
But be."2
Translator’s note

The page closes by giving one line each to the two poets it footnotes. The first is Szymborska's, from her 1996 Nobel lecture — in her own English, "whatever inspiration is, it's born from a continuous I don't know." The Persian keeps دائم, "continually," but swaps her born for می‌روید, the plant-verb that runs through the rest of this page, so here inspiration grows out of not-knowing rather than being born from it. The second is MacLeish, the close of Ars Poetica, restored to his own words rather than translated back out of the Persian.

Mohammad Ebrahim Jafari, 2017
1 Wisława Szymborska, Polish poet, winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature, (1923–2012).
2 Archibald MacLeish, American poet and librarian, (1892–1982).
