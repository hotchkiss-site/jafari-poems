٢ آرچیبالد مک‌لیش، شاعر و کتابدار آمریکایی (۱۸۹۲–۱۹۸۲)
English The shortest ghazal I know is the weeping willow.
Translator’s note

بید مجنون (bid-e majnun), the Persian name for the weeping willow, is literally Majnun's willow — named for the madman-lover of Layli. The tree is already a love poem: a name out of a romance, a shape that hangs like a lament. English keeps the weeping and loses the lover.

I have heard that art is a life intensified. Love begins with a gaze intensified — a gaze that shines, a gaze that blazes.1 Drink today's wonder to the last drop and tomorrow you will be thirstier still. Don't leave a single breath of today for tomorrow. Whoever breathes in yesterday has no new song today.2 Between tradition and the new there stands a bridge, and it is paced out on the feet of ardor.
From my soul's coupling with ardor,
in the wake of a heat and a fever, writhing,
a shadow grows
behind the curtain of nothing…
Translator’s note

روییدن (ruidan) is the verb for a plant coming up, and it is this book's quiet refrain: a shadow grows here; two maxims on, out of I do not know grows I know; and in Farrokhi's introduction a peasant's fortune has grown like a shadow beside his feet. It is given as "grow" in all three. The Persian also rhymes پیچاپیچ (coil upon coil) with هیچ (nothing) — writhing / nothing is a reach for that chime.

When you look at reality with a new eye, reality turns into an occasion.
Translator’s note

واقعیت (vâqe'iyat, reality) and واقعه (vâqe'e, event) are one root, Arabic و-ق-ع, "to fall, to befall": reality is what has fallen out, an event is what falls. English can hold the pun only by falling in Latin — case, occasion, incident, accident all come down from cadere, "to fall." Hence "occasion" rather than "event."
