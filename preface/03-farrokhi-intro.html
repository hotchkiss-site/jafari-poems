فارسی استاد محمد ابراهیم جعفری صورتگر و نقاش بزرگ را شعرهایی است که دوشادوش تکامل او در نقش‌بندی سرایش یافته و از به دفتر نشستن آن‌ها دوری جسته است. سرانجام اما ابرام همدم همدل و همسر فرزانهٔ وی، بانو مریم... و دوستان نزدیک به چاپ این نخستین دفتر انجامید. جعفری در نقاشی تثبیت‌کنندهٔ داده‌های سوبژکتیو لحظه‌های زندگی‌ست و در شعرهای خویش سخنگوی این لحظه‌ها... و چنین است که بسیاری از سروده‌هایش با نقش‌بندهایش پهلو می‌زند و گاه یک و همان است.
English Master Mohammad Ebrahim Jafari — the great image-maker and painter — has poems that were composed shoulder to shoulder with his growth in the binding of patterns, and that he kept from ever settling into a notebook. In the end, though, the insistence of his kindred companion and wise wife, Lady Maryam... and of close friends, brought this first volume to print. In painting, Jafari is the one who fixes fast the subjective data of life's moments; in his poems he is the spokesman of those moments — and so it is that many of his verses stand level with his paintings, and at times are one and the same.
Translator’s note

Two words for painter run through this introduction: صورتگر (surat-gar), a maker of forms and faces, and نقش‌بند (naqsh-band), a binder of patterns — the same word that names the Naqshbandi Sufi order. Farrokhi's claim, that the composing and the painting are one act, is already inside his vocabulary, so the English keeps the compounds standing: image-maker, the binding of patterns.

شعرهای کوتاه این دفتر در خوانش چنان چون ضرب‌آهنگ قلم‌مویی در خلق یک اثر می‌باشد و غنای آن در مرحلهٔ نخست یادآور تک‌بیت‌های شگفت‌انگیز شاعران کلاسیک پارسی و گاه هایکوهای ژرف ژاپنی است؛ و در کار شعر همانند نقاشی نه پیرو دیگران که متکی بر خویشتن خویش است. صورتگران شاعر در کار خویش باریک‌بین و بی‌همتا هستند — در سرزمین‌های دیگر نیز می‌توان از ژنی‌هایی چون وانگ وی، نقاش و شاعر چینی (۶۳۷–۷۴۰ میلادی)، و میکل‌آنژ، پیکرتراش، نقاش و شاعر ایتالیایی، یاد کرد.

In the reading, the short poems of this volume are like the rhythm of a brush bringing a work into being; their richness, at first encounter, recalls the astonishing single couplets of the classical Persian poets, and at times the profound Japanese haiku. And in the work of poetry, as in painting, he follows no one but his own self. Painter-poets are fine-eyed and without peer in their work — in other lands too one can name geniuses like Wang Wei, the Chinese painter and poet (637–740 AD), and Michelangelo, the Italian sculptor, painter, and poet.

am silent

— as if the moon, for its part, has come to the poet's aid: to speak in fellow-feeling of everything he leaves unsaid, and by becoming one with him to say that silence and the night are brimming with the unsaid.

And this poem by Wang Wei, in Persian rendering:
Alone, I play the oud in a far reed-bed;
under my breath I whistle a tune.
In the endless reeds there is no one.
The moon — and its shining rays.
And this poem by Bashō (1644–1694 AD), from the Persian of Shamlou and Pashaei:
Clouds
give a moment's rest
to the moon-gazers.
Translator’s note

Both of these reach English by way of Persian, and the journey shows. Wang Wei's poem is the "Bamboo Grove" (竹里館): in the Chinese he sits in a dark bamboo grove and plays a qin, a zither; in the Persian the bamboo has become a نیزار, a reed-bed, and the zither an عود — and since نی, reed, is also the Persian flute, the instrument turns up twice in a poem about playing alone. The Bashō arrives through Ahmad Shamlou and A. Pashaei's Persian versions. Wang Wei's dates are given here as 637–740; they are usually 699–759.

And this poem by Jafari, which is an astonishing painting:
Fifty years now
this turtledove
has sat on a branch heaped with snow.
— in which time finds its way to timelessness, and the turtledove to eternity.

And again, this poem of Jafari's:
When I weep in memory of you, my tears are blue…
I love you a sky's love —
for love is blue, and so is the sky.

And this poem — a grievous memorial to the Rudbar earthquake of 1369 (1990):
On the night of the earthquake…
the moon, in the eye of a firefly,
turned to a rain of moonlight… and…
poured over the rubble.
The firefly had not the strength to hear
the most grief-stirring chorus
from a people who
had never sung so with one heart.
Until… the last breath…
— a grievous tableau, a painful dirge, until… the last breath….
سروده‌های بلند جعفری همانند شعرهای کوتاه وی و تابلوهای شگفت‌انگیزش، تو در توست. شعر او پیوسته نیست و همانند بسیاری از شاعران ماندگار، در گذر زمان و با توجه به تاریخ سروده‌ها، از رمانتیک تا شعر روز، از نظر شکل و محتوا دگرگون می‌شود — که ذات جعفری صورتگر و شاعر ایستا نیست و مدام در شدن است:

دهقانی است با چوبدست بلندش
سپید، سیاه
خرش را می‌گویم
با خرواری از خارهای باران‌زده
سیاه سیاه...

بختش را می‌گویم.
که همچون سایه در کنار پاهایش روییده است.
که تابلویی است از دهقانی با جامهٔ آبی، شلوار خاکستری، و خر سیاه‌وسفید، بارکش با باری از خارهای باران‌خورده برای افروختن هیزم اجاق و تنور دیگران — نماد فقر روستا و...
Jafari's long poems, like his short poems and his astonishing paintings, are fold within fold. His poetry is not all of a piece; like that of many enduring poets it changes over time — and the change can be tracked by the dates of the poems, from the romantic to the verse of the day — shifting in form and in content alike. For the essence of Jafari, image-maker and poet, is not static: it is forever in the act of becoming:
On the back roads —
blue, gray, black —
a peasant with his long staff.
White, black.
His donkey, I mean —
under a donkey-load of rain-beaten thorns.
Black, black…

His fortune, I mean —
which has grown like a shadow beside his feet.
→ road-peasant
— a tableau of a peasant in a blue robe and gray trousers with a black-and-white pack donkey, carrying a load of rain-beaten thorns to kindle the fire in other people's hearths and bread ovens: a symbol of village poverty, and…

دیوانه‌وار
تا که بمیرم به دامنت

که در آن ریزش انوار خورشید بر تنهٔ آبشار و قطره‌های رنگارنگ آب چون برق، تیر نگاه، و امواج سینهٔ آبشار به سپیدی ماه است — و در شوق یکی شدن، قله تا دریا را در می‌نوردد. با رسیدن به دریا در آرزوی بازگشت به دره‌های دور و قله است — چرخه‌ای که آرزوی بازگشت به آغاز را مطرح می‌سازد. شعری است که حدود شصت سال پیش سروده شده و از شعر شاعران آن روزگار اگر فراتر نباشد با همان سبک و سیاق با آن‌ها پهلو می‌زند. گویای جانی همیشه شاعر و مداماً هوشیار است — احساس فرو شدن در طبیعت، یگانه شدن با آبشار، آرزوی فرو شدن در دریای بی‌پایان، و باز آرزوی بازگشت به قله.
And the tableau-poem of the waterfall:
I am that blithe, drunken waterfall
who for years
wept on the warm sands of your body,
like a madman,
and to die in your lap
leapt free of the trap of my own white snows.
→ drunk-waterfall
Translator’s note

The Persian closes on two sounds at once: دامنت (dâman-at, your skirt — and also a mountain's skirt, which is where a waterfall actually ends) against دام (dâm, snare), and ریختم (I poured out) against گریختم (I fled). English can hold one pair — lap against trap — and "leapt" is offered for the fleeing, since leaping is what a waterfall does with its freedom.

In it the sun's rays fall on the body of the waterfall, the many-colored droplets like lightning, like the arrow of a glance, and the waves of the waterfall's breast are white as the moon — and in its ardor to become one, it spans peak to sea. Reaching the sea, it longs for the distant valleys and the summit again — a cycle that raises the wish to return to the beginning. It is a poem written some sixty years ago, and in the very same style and idiom it stands level with the verse of the poets of that age, if it does not go beyond them. It bespeaks a soul forever a poet and perpetually awake — the sense of sinking into nature, of becoming one with the waterfall, the wish to sink into the endless sea, and again the wish to return to the peak.

The love poems in this volume are not few, for many of these showings rise from a soul forever in love — and in their highest aspect this is no erotic love, but a love for the human being and for all that is:
I love you —
this surmise,
O ancient, undying anthem,
this leaven kneaded by the angels of heaven,
this certainty,
this oldest song of the earth —
like the color of cloud,
like the smell of clay,
like the taste of tears,
like the sleep of mountains, it goes on always…
→ oldest-song
Translator’s note

سرشته (kneaded, moulded) chimes against فرشته (angel) in the Persian. "Leaven … heaven" is a reach for a rhyme in the same place, and it keeps the dough: سرشتن is what a baker does.

And here and there in this volume, again, you see the great image-maker longing to turn inward — to raise a peerless tableau out of his own soul, and to make his singing and his brushwork one and the same:
Would that I were a ruined tower on a far road,
so that on rainy nights
the rain-damp pigeons, under the roof of my veranda,
would lean on one leg and sleep.
…
At the foot of the veranda, beside the burning fire,
two grief-worn night watchmen
told each other all that had befallen them.
→ old-news
که جعفری در هنر، تمام دنیایش پنجره‌ای است دستی و حنجره‌ای توانمند — که در این قلعهٔ پیر بس سخن‌های ناگفته دارد، در تجانس آوایی و تجسم کلماتش و رنگین‌کمان رنگ‌ها و نقش‌بندی‌هایش.

بگذار خواننده را بر خوانش سروده‌های این دفتر واگذاریم و چونان هوکوسایی باشیم که آرزویش خلاصه کردن همهٔ آثار خود در نقطه‌ای بود، و بس.
For Jafari, in art, his whole world is a window made by hand and a throat of great power — and in this aged fortress he keeps many an unspoken word, in the kinship of his sounds and the bodying-forth of his words, and in the rainbow of his colors and his patterns.

Let us leave the reader to the reading of this volume's poems, and be like Hokusai, whose wish was to sum up all of his work in a single dot — and no more.
باجلان فرخی (م.ح)، اردیبهشت ۱۳۹۶
