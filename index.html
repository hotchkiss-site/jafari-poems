فارسی کوتاه‌ترین غزلی که می‌شناسم «بید مجنون» است. شنیده‌ام: هنر حیاتی شدت یافته است. عشق با نگاه شدّت‌یافته آغاز می‌شود، نگاهی درخشنده و تابناک. اگر شگفتی امروز را تا آخرین قطره بنوشی، فردا تشنه‌تر خواهی بود. یک نفس از امروز را به فردا مگذار، آن که در دیروز نفس می‌کشد، امروز آوازی تازه ندارد. میان سنت و نوگرایی پلی است که با پاهای شوق پیموده می‌شود.
از هم‌آغوشی جانم با شوق
در پی تاب و تبی پیچاپیچ
سایه‌ای می‌روید
در پسِ پردهٔ هیچ...
وقتی نگاهی نو به واقعیت می‌کنی، واقعیت به واقعه تبدیل می‌شود. باغ شعر من در این سایه‌ها گل داده است. من باور دارم که...

١ ویساوا شیمبورسکا، شاعر لهستانی، برندهٔ جایزهٔ نوبل ادبیات، (۱۹۲۳–۲۰۱۲).
٢ آرچی بالد مکلیش، شاعر و کتابدار امریکایی، (۱۸۹۲–۱۹۸۲).
English The shortest ghazal I know is the weeping willow.
Translator’s note

بید مجنون (bid-e majnun), the Persian name for the weeping willow, is literally Majnun's willow — named for the madman-lover of Layli. The tree is already a love poem: a name out of a romance, a shape that hangs like a lament. English keeps the weeping and loses the lover.

I have heard it said: art is a life intensified. Love begins with a gaze intensified — a gaze that shines, a gaze that blazes. Drink today's wonder to the last drop and tomorrow you will be thirstier still. Don't leave a single breath of today for tomorrow. Whoever breathes in yesterday has no new song today. Between tradition and the new there stands a bridge, and it is paced out on the feet of ardor.
From my soul's coupling with ardor,
in the wake of a heat and a fever, writhing,
a shadow grows
behind the curtain of nothing…
Translator’s note

روییدن (ruidan) is the verb for a plant coming up, and it is this page's own refrain: a shadow grows here; inspiration grows continually out of I do not know at the foot of the page; and in Farrokhi's introduction a peasant's fortune has grown like a shadow beside his feet. It is given as "grow" in all three. The Persian also rhymes پیچاپیچ (coil upon coil) with هیچ (nothing) — writhing / nothing is a reach for that chime.

When you look at reality with a new eye, reality turns into an occasion.
Translator’s note

واقعیت (vâqe'iyat, reality) and واقعه (vâqe'e, event) are one root, Arabic و-ق-ع, "to fall, to befall": reality is what has fallen out, an event is what falls. English can hold the pun only by falling in Latin — case, occasion, incident, accident all come down from cadere, "to fall." Hence "occasion" rather than "event."

My garden of poetry has come into flower in these shadows. I believe that…
"Whatever inspiration may be, it grows continually out of I do not know."1
I believe that…
"A poem should not mean
But be."2
Translator’s note

The page closes by giving one line each to the two poets it footnotes. The first is Szymborska's, from her 1996 Nobel lecture — in her own English, "whatever inspiration is, it's born from a continuous I don't know." The Persian keeps دائم, "continually," but swaps her born for می‌روید, the plant-verb that runs through the rest of this page, so here inspiration grows out of not-knowing rather than being born from it. The second is MacLeish, the close of Ars Poetica, restored to his own words rather than translated back out of the Persian.

Mohammad Ebrahim Jafari, 2017
1 Wisława Szymborska, Polish poet, winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature, (1923–2012).
2 Archibald MacLeish, American poet and librarian, (1892–1982).
سرخی دانهٔ انار — پیشگفتار خسرو سیناییThe Redness of the Pomegranate Seed — Preface by Khosrow Sinaiاردیبهشت ۱۳۹۶ / Spring 2017
فارسی بالاخره پس از سال‌ها تأخیر، آنچه که باید، اتفاق افتاد. من فقط خوشحال نیستم، ذوق‌زده‌ام. خوب می‌دانم که این ذوق‌زدگی فقط از سر رفاقت من با محمدابراهیم جعفری نیست. یادم هست چند سال پیش فردی منتقد از من پرسید: «شما فکر می‌کنی خودت هنرمندی؟!» جواب دادم: «نمی‌دانم!»

...اما خوب می‌دانم که حدود شصت‌سال با انواع هنرمندان ارتباط دوستی دور و نزدیک داشته‌ام، شاعر... نقاش... موسیقیدان... و بالاخره فیلمساز... و آموخته‌ام تشخیص دهم که چه کسی ذاتاً هنرمند است و چه‌کسی با باد به غبغب انداختن و تفرعن و نوچه‌پروری آثارش را به مردمان تحمیل می‌کند. طی همهٔ این سال‌ها چه نادر بودند کسانی که ذات هنرمندشان، مرا به شگفتی واداشت، و بی‌تردید محمدابراهیم جعفری یکی از آن‌هاست.
English At last, after years of delay, the thing that had to happen has happened. I am not merely glad — I am struck with delight. And I know well that this delight is not simply a matter of my comradeship with Mohammad Ebrahim Jafari. I remember a critic asking me, a few years ago: "Do you think you yourself are an artist?!" I answered: "I don't know!"

...But this much I do know: for some sixty years I have kept friendships, near and far, with artists of every kind — poets... painters... musicians... and at last filmmakers — and I have learned to tell the one who is an artist by his very nature from the one who puffs out his jowls, plays the pharaoh, breeds a retinue of yes-men, and forces his work on people. In all those years, how rare were the ones whose artist's nature struck me with wonder — and Mohammad Ebrahim Jafari is, beyond doubt, one of them.
طنزگزندهٔ او در بعضی از اشعارش همیشه مرا در فضایی میان اشعار عبید زاکانی و ایرج‌میرزا قرار می‌داد. یادم نمی‌رود در آن شب‌هایی که بر سرمان بمب و موشک می‌بارید، او در جمع دوستان دوتارش را برمی‌داشت، ضرب‌آهنگی را تکرار می‌کرد و با صدایی خش‌دار برایمان می‌خواند: «جنگلا پُر تَمشکه!» و ما دوستان در آن شب‌های غم‌آلود، با احساسی میان گریه و خنده با او همدل و همزبان و هم‌آواز می‌شدیم. مگر نه این که او در پایان شعری گفته:
«گاه خندان می‌گریم...
گاه گریان می‌خندم...
عاقلان می‌گویند:...
من هنرمندم!»
بعدها از روش آوازخوانی‌اش که ایرانی بود، محلی بود، غیرمتعارف بود، اما اصیل و امروزی بود، کسانی برداشت کردند، به شهرت رسیدند و کمتر کسی دانست که او آغازگر آن راه بود.
The biting satire of some of his poems always set me somewhere between the verse of Obeyd Zakani and Iraj Mirza. I will never forget those nights when bombs and missiles rained down on our heads: among friends he would take up his dutar, work a rhythm over and over, and sing to us in that raspy voice — "The woods are full o' brambleberries!" And on those grief-laden nights we friends, with a feeling caught between weeping and laughter, became of one heart, one tongue, one voice with him. Did he not say, at the close of one poem:
"Sometimes I weep for laughing…
sometimes I laugh for weeping…
The sane ones have a word for it:
I am an artist!"
Translator’s note

Obeyd Zakani (d. c. 1370) and Iraj Mirza (1874–1926) are the great satirists of Persian verse — the first scabrous and obscene, the second a Qajar prince who wrote in the plainest street idiom. To be placed "between" them is to be placed in a line of poets who mock power and piety with a straight face. عاقلان is "the sane ones," those in possession of عقل, reason — and the joke of the last line is that sanity's diagnosis for a man who laughs while weeping is artist.

Later, others took up his way of singing — Iranian, regional, unconventional, and yet rooted and of today — and rose to fame, and few knew that he had been the one to open that road.
گاه فروتنی و صمیمیت ترانه‌هایش مرا به یاد باباطاهر می‌اندازد. وقتی می‌گوید:
دوتا کفتر سفید و زعفرونی
نشستن دِ تثار سایه‌بونی
سفیده ناله سو... سیل شفق کرد
سه کو... آفتو مِزه مِثه جوونی
گاه در رندی و ظرافت اشعارش همان جنس وجود شاعری را می‌یابم که بزرگی چون حافظ را به تاریخ فرهنگمان هدیه کرد. او همیشه در ذهن من تروبادورهای قرون وسطای اروپا را تداعی می‌کند، همان خوانندگان دوره‌گردی که آوازخوان از شهری به شهری می‌رفتند، و سرودن جزئی از بودنشان بود.
Sometimes the humility and the warmth of his songs put me in mind of Baba Taher. When he says:
Two pigeons, one white, one saffron,
settled in the shade of an arbor.
The white one gave a moan… and looked at the red of the dawn.
Look — the sun tastes of youth.
Translator’s note

A do-bayti in rural dialect — the form Baba Taher made his own, which is why Sinai reaches for his name. Two dialect facts carry it: سیل کردن (sil kardan) in Lori and Bakhtiari means to look, not "to flood," so it is the dove that looks at the dawn and not the dawn that floods; and آفتو، مِزه، مِثه are the dialect's آفتاب، مزه، مثل — sun, taste, like. The last two lines are damaged in the source: "look —" for سه کو follows the looking of the line before it, and is a guess.

And sometimes, in the rendi and the fineness of his verse, I find that same stuff of poetic being which made a gift of Hafez to the history of our culture. He has always called to my mind the troubadours of medieval Europe — those wandering singers who went from town to town in song, and for whom composing was a part of their very being.
Translator’s note

رند (rend) is Hafez's own word for his own kind: the wine-loving, hypocrisy-piercing free spirit whose disreputability is a form of integrity. "Rogue," "libertine," "rake" each catch a third of him, so the Persian is left standing here — Sinai names Hafez in the same sentence, and the word is half of his argument.

همیشه به او گفته‌ام که ذهن شاعرانه آنچنان در تو می‌جوشد، که چون فرزندانی که قدر ثروت پدری را نمی‌دانند، ثروت ذاتی وجودت را چنان مُسرفانه و با فروتنی ارائه می‌کنی، که آن‌ها که از حساسیت هنرمندانه تو بی‌بهره‌اند، ممکن است به‌سادگی از آن‌ها بگذرند و در انتظار شاعرانی! بمانند که باد به غیبغب می‌اندازند و آنچه را که نه خود می‌فهمند و نه مخاطبان، با آب‌وتاب به دیگران حُقنه می‌کنند.

اگر بپذیریم که جنین شعر در ذهن شاعر جان می‌گیرد و رشد می‌کند؛ آنچه که آن را به بیرون از ذهن متجلی می‌کند، یعنی به مخاطب منتقل می‌کند، جز ابزاری نیست که شاعر بر آن تسلط دارد، و این ابزار کلام می‌تواند باشد، موسیقی باشد، نقاشی باشد، و یا هر هنر دیگری که عامل خلاقیت شاعرانه در آن نقشی دارد. جعفری طی سال‌ها از دو گونه ابزار استفاده کرده است: کلام و نقاشی، که در دومی سال‌ها در مقام استاد به پرورش نسل‌های جوان پرداخته است. یکی از شاگردانش که امروز خود نقاش نامداری‌ست، روزی درباره او به من گفت: «اغلب اساتید به ما فنون نقاشی را می‌آموختند، اما او علاوه‌بر آن روح هنرمند را در ما بیدار می‌کرد.»
I have always told him: the poetic mind wells up in you so abundantly that — like children who don't know what their father's wealth is worth — you spend the wealth native to your being so prodigally, and so humbly, that those who lack your artist's sensitivity may pass it by, and go on waiting for "poets"! who puff out their jowls and, with much fanfare, force down other people's throats what neither they nor their listeners understand.

If we grant that the embryo of a poem takes life and grows in the poet's mind, then whatever brings it out of that mind — whatever carries it to another person — is nothing but an instrument the poet has mastered. That instrument may be the word, it may be music, it may be painting, or any other art in which the poetic creative force has a part to play. Over the years Jafari has used two instruments, word and painting, and in the second he spent years as a master, bringing on the young. One of his students, himself a renowned painter today, once said to me of him: "Most of our teachers taught us the techniques of painting; he, on top of that, woke the artist's soul in us."
راست می‌گفت! به‌عنوان دوستش همیشه او را چون سیم ظریف سه‌تاری شناخته‌ام که وقتی مرتعش می‌شود، فقط سیم‌هایی را به ارتعاش درمی‌آورد که از ظرافتی مشابه برخوردارند. همین یک شعر او نمونه‌ای از گواهان بسیار این گفتهٔ من است:
دل من انار هزاردانه‌ای‌ست
با توان سرخ عشقی
در هر دانه...
وقتی تو به من نگاه می‌کنی
یکی از دانه‌ها سرخ‌تر می‌شود.
اعتراف می‌کنم که به‌عنوان یکی از اهالی هنر، گاهی به جوشش شاعرانهٔ ذهن او غبطه خورده‌ام.
He was right. As his friend I have always known him as the fine string of a setar — the one that, when it is set trembling, wakes into vibration only those strings that share its delicacy. This single poem of his is one witness among many to what I mean:
My heart is a pomegranate of a thousand seeds,
a love's red strength
in every seed…
When you look at me,
one of the seeds turns redder.
I confess that, as one of art's own people, I have at times felt an admiring envy at the poetic welling of his mind.
خوشحالم که بالاخره به‌کوشش همسر گرامی‌اش، پذیرفت تا اشعارش، که به باور من از زلال‌ترین اشعار زمانهٔ ما هستند، به گسترهٔ وسیع‌تری از مخاطبان ارائه شود، تا پی‌ببرند که چگونه می‌توان بدون سردرگم کردن مخاطب در پیچیدگی جملات نامفهوم و هزارتوی نظریه‌های گنگ، ایرانی بود، اصیل بود، امروزی بود... و شاعر بود!
I am glad that at last, through the efforts of his dear wife, he agreed to let his poems — which I hold to be among the most limpid verse of our age — reach a wider range of readers, so that they may see how it is possible, without losing anyone in a tangle of unintelligible sentences and a thousand-fold labyrinth of inarticulate theory, to be Iranian, to be rooted, to be of today… and to be a poet!
خسرو سینایی، اردیبهشت ۱۳۹۶

فارسی استاد محمد ابراهیم جعفری صورتگر و نقاش بزرگ را شعرهایی است که دوشادوش تکامل او در نقش‌بندی سرایش یافته و از به دفتر نشستن آن‌ها دوری جسته است. سرانجام اما ابرام همدم همدل و همسر فرزانهٔ وی، بانو مریم... و دوستان نزدیک به چاپ این نخستین دفتر انجامید. جعفری در نقاشی تثبیت‌کنندهٔ داده‌های سوبژکتیو لحظه‌های زندگی‌ست و در شعرهای خویش سخنگوی این لحظه‌ها... و چنین است که بسیاری از سروده‌هایش با نقش‌بندهایش پهلو می‌زند و گاه یک و همان است.
English Master Mohammad Ebrahim Jafari — the great image-maker and painter — has poems that were composed shoulder to shoulder with his growth in the binding of patterns, and that he kept from ever settling into a notebook. In the end, though, the insistence of his kindred companion and wise wife, Lady Maryam... and of close friends, brought this first volume to print. In painting, Jafari is the one who fixes fast the subjective data of life's moments; in his poems he is the spokesman of those moments — and so it is that many of his verses stand level with his paintings, and at times are one and the same.
Translator’s note

Two words for painter run through this introduction: صورتگر (surat-gar), a maker of forms and faces, and نقش‌بند (naqsh-band), a binder of patterns — the same word that names the Naqshbandi Sufi order. Farrokhi's claim, that the composing and the painting are one act, is already inside his vocabulary, so the English keeps the compounds standing: image-maker, the binding of patterns.

شعرهای کوتاه این دفتر در خوانش چنان چون ضرب‌آهنگ قلم‌مویی در خلق یک اثر می‌باشد و غنای آن در مرحلهٔ نخست یادآور تک‌بیت‌های شگفت‌انگیز شاعران کلاسیک پارسی و گاه هایکوهای ژرف ژاپنی است؛ و در کار شعر همانند نقاشی نه پیرو دیگران که متکی بر خویشتن خویش است. صورتگران شاعر در کار خویش باریک‌بین و بی‌همتا هستند — در سرزمین‌های دیگر نیز می‌توان از ژنی‌هایی چون وانگ وی، نقاش و شاعر چینی (۶۳۷–۷۴۰ میلادی)، و میکل‌آنژ، پیکرتراش، نقاش و شاعر ایتالیایی، یاد کرد.

In the reading, the short poems of this volume are like the rhythm of a brush bringing a work into being; their richness, at first encounter, recalls the astonishing single couplets of the classical Persian poets, and at times the profound Japanese haiku. And in the work of poetry, as in painting, he follows no one but his own self. Painter-poets are fine-eyed and without peer in their work — in other lands too one can name geniuses like Wang Wei, the Chinese painter and poet (637–740 AD), and Michelangelo, the Italian sculptor, painter, and poet.

And this poem by Wang Wei, in Persian rendering:
Alone, I play the oud in a far reed-bed;
under my breath I whistle a tune.
In the endless reeds there is no one.
The moon — and its shining rays.
And this poem by Bashō (1644–1694 AD), from the Persian of Shamlou and Pashaei:
Clouds
give a moment's rest
to the moon-gazers.
Translator’s note

Both of these reach English by way of Persian, and the journey shows. Wang Wei's poem is the "Bamboo Grove" (竹里館): in the Chinese he sits in a dark bamboo grove and plays a qin, a zither; in the Persian the bamboo has become a نیزار, a reed-bed, and the zither an عود — and since نی, reed, is also the Persian flute, the instrument turns up twice in a poem about playing alone. The Bashō arrives through Ahmad Shamlou and A. Pashaei's Persian versions. Wang Wei's dates are given here as 637–740; they are usually 699–759.

And this poem by Jafari, which is an astonishing painting:
Fifty years now
this turtledove
has sat on a branch heaped with snow.
— in which time finds its way to timelessness, and the turtledove to eternity.

And again, this poem of Jafari's:
When I weep in memory of you, my tears are blue…
I love you a sky's love —
for love is blue, and so is the sky.

Jafari's long poems, like his short poems and his astonishing paintings, are fold within fold. His poetry is not all of a piece; like that of many enduring poets it changes over time — and the change can be tracked by the dates of the poems, from the romantic to the verse of the day — shifting in form and in content alike. For the essence of Jafari, image-maker and poet, is not static: it is forever in the act of becoming:
On the back roads —

And the tableau-poem of the waterfall:
I am that blithe, drunken waterfall
who for years
wept on the warm sands of your body,
like a madman,
and to die in your lap
leapt free of the trap of my own white snows.
→ drunk-waterfall
Translator’s note

The Persian closes on two sounds at once: دامنت (dâman-at, your skirt — and also a mountain's skirt, which is where a waterfall actually ends) against دام (dâm, snare), and ریختم (I poured out) against گریختم (I fled). English can hold one pair — lap against trap — and "leapt" is offered for the fleeing, since leaping is what a waterfall does with its freedom.

In it the sun's rays fall on the body of the waterfall, the many-colored droplets like lightning, like the arrow of a glance, and the waves of the waterfall's breast are white as the moon — and in its ardor to become one, it spans peak to sea. Reaching the sea, it longs for the distant valleys and the summit again — a cycle that raises the wish to return to the beginning. It is a poem written some sixty years ago, and in the very same style and idiom it stands level with the verse of the poets of that age, if it does not go beyond them. It bespeaks a soul forever a poet and perpetually awake — the sense of sinking into nature, of becoming one with the waterfall, the wish to sink into the endless sea, and again the wish to return to the peak.

For Jafari, in art, his whole world is a window made by hand and a throat of great power — and in this aged fortress he keeps many an unspoken word, in the kinship of his sounds and the bodying-forth of his words, and in the rainbow of his colors and his patterns.

Let us leave the reader to the reading of this volume's poems, and be like Hokusai, whose wish was to sum up all of his work in a single dot — and no more.

Translator’s note

This page is in Jafari's own hand, and it is where the book gets its name. Its last three lines are بوی کاهگل / آواز پرنده را / پررنگ‌تر می‌کند — the smell of adobe deepens the colour of the bird's song — and the title on the cover is lifted from them. Note that the Persian is not a pairing of two nice things but a claim about one acting on the other. The same sentence comes back in the long dream-letter to Hossein Alizadeh, where Alizadeh is the one who says it.

The page is a fair copy, in his hand, of the poem printed on page 72 of the book, which stands in this collection as adobe-birdsong. Reading the two together resolves nearly all of what was unreadable here: the printed text is what identifies صدای کبوترانم (the voices of my doves), دوتار پیری (an aged dotar) and رنگین (gave it colour). Two places where this manuscript departs from the print are kept as the manuscript has them — کی ترا شناختم, a line the printed page does not carry, and تو به توی, "fold upon fold," which is very likely the answer to a crux in the print, where the same phrase is set as توبه تو and reads as "the walls of your repentance."

An earlier English here, built on a corrupt OCR of this page, opened "tonight I painted the bruise-blue sound of my song." That was a mirage: صدای کبوترانم, the voices of his doves, had been split into صدای کبود ترانم. In the same way من با دوتار پیری became برین باد و کارپوری, رنگین became زانی, and بوی کاهگل — the book's own title — became بودی کامنگل. The doubled کاش باران ببارد, which had been dismissed here as a scanning artifact, is real: the poet writes the line twice.

Translator's Notes

جنگل (jangal) is rendered "forest" rather than "jungle": Jafari's recurring war-poem image is the wooded Caspian littoral, a place of refuge between the mountains and the sea. The same sea/forest pairing returns in the page-65 poem ("still the forest is bound to the sea"). The dateline places this in Isfahan, spring 1367 (May 1988), in the closing months of the Iran–Iraq war.

The contrast turns on a single verb: گریست ("wept," third-person singular) — it is the other listener, not the poet, who weeps. So the poem you laughed at, read aloud to someone else, drew tears. Four lines, two reactions, and the whole distance between them.

I kept the suspension rather than resolving it into a clean sentence. فاصله ("distance, gap, interval") is the same word that opens the page-61 poem; here the gap is between two breaths.

nightingale-cloud

Rendered
متن اصلی

هوای ابر را بلبل نداره به جز شوق گل و سنبل نداره نمی‌دونه اگه بارون نباره گل پژمرده بوی گل نداره ۱۳۸۰

Translation

Nightingale couldn't weather a cloud he only ever took a shine to hyacinth & rose he doesn't know - if the rain won't rain, the wilted rose is but a scentless rose.

Translator's Notes

The Persian is a do-baytī — a folk quatrain in colloquial speech (نداره، بارون), the register of Bābā Tāher's songs — and its first line turns on هوا: at once "weather" and "craving." The nightingale has no *havā* for clouds. No single English word holds both senses, so this translation scatters the pun across three — "weather," "took a shine," "rain won't rain." Read it aloud with a slight Irish lilt: Hiberno-English keeps "took a shine to" and "is but" in one living voice, much as the original's dialect carries its old forms unselfconsciously.

Translator's Notes

خطّی و آونگی — *linear, and pendulous.* Before the poem will say what is hanging there, it says what a painter sees: a line, and a weight swinging from it. That refusal to name the bodies for two lines is the whole method of the poem, and the reason the horror arrives when it does. دهن‌درّه is a *yawn* — literally a tearing-open of the mouth. The breeze carries the smell of the carcasses not to the hyenas' jaws but to their yawning, which is worse: they are bored. And when the wind rises hard enough, what they fail to do is *recognise* the crucified man by his long hair — مصلوب and صلیب put a crucifixion in a book that otherwise has none, so the long hair invites the reading it invites. The wind finishes the job the hyenas could not, and carries the empty cross off the hill.

Translator's Notes

هذیان is the raving of fever or shell-shock, and بعد از جنگ dates it: ۱۳۶۸ is 1989, a year after the August 1988 ceasefire. **So there is no raid.** The poem announces itself as a symptom and then performs one — the counterfactual comes back word for word, the way a delirious man repeats himself, and the closing line is a reflex firing after the danger has stopped. What breaks off the tenderness is not a siren but the war still running inside him. پناهگاه is the same bomb-shelter as the page-60 poem, which was written while the shelter was still needed. **Why the rain would be a different colour.** The counterfactual is a painter's, and it is doing more than it appears to. Rain has no colour of its own; it takes the sky's — which is why rain against an overcast looks grey and rain at sunset looks gold. Tears are rain on a face, so they borrow too. The chain runs: the clouds are grey, so the rain is grey, so her weeping is grey — and if the sky were anything else, the same tears would come out سبز کم‌رنگ, a pale green, which in this book is the colour of longing (page 88, "the green of longing"). He is saying that grief takes its colour from the weather and the weather is war: under this sky there is not even longing left in her crying. And he cannot change the sky. He is a painter, so the only way he has of saying *the war has ruined us* is to say *the grey has ruined the rain* — which the next line then states outright: رنگ جنگ در آه می‌ماند, the colour of war stays. The Persian turns to sound at exactly the point where it states that finding. رنگ جنگ (*rang-e jang*, "the colour of war") rhymes inside itself, and the line then closes on آه … نگاه (*âh … negâh*) — four rhyming syllables in one line. English can have one of the two: *stays in the sigh, stays in the eye*, taking نگاه as the eye rather than the gaze, which the next line's چشم‌ها is about to do regardless. چشم‌ها and دندان‌ها are bare plurals — "in eyes," "in teeth," with no possessive. Not *their* eyes, not *our* teeth: an anatomy, and a diagnosis. Eyes are where a thing is received and teeth are where it is bared, so the body is split, one half sick of the war and the other half unable to stop. That someone should hate *the peace*, a year after it arrived, is the coldest line in the book. **And the grey has ash in it.** خاکستری is خاکستر — ash — with an adjective ending: Persian's ordinary word for the colour is *ash-coloured*, the way English "orange" is a fruit nobody tastes any more. Two things make that worth saying here. The poem's subject is what war leaves behind, and ash is precisely residue. And خاکستر is built on خاک, clay — the word this book renders "clay," and the word standing in its own title, بوی کاهگل, the smell of straw-and-clay. Adobe is what the walls are made of; ash is what adobe becomes when the house burns. The smell the book is named for and the colour the war leaves behind are the same material, before and after fire. *Ash-grey* for خاکستری was weighed and not taken. It is defensible on the ground just given, but the poem wants to make its own reveal: for five lines this may be weather, and then line six tells you it is war. Ash in the second line arrives with the answer already inside it. Grey is also flat, and flatness is how delirium sounds — every other word in this poem is ordinary (clouds, rain, tears, eyes, teeth, moonlight, shelter), so a compound would be the one literary thing on the page. The etymology is here instead, for whoever wants it. خاکستری is also the poet's own colour, loved outright on page 76 ("I love the greys, the greys…") and turned against him here.

Translator's Notes

The colour of cloud, the smell of earth, the taste of tears: Jafari had used this exact triad some twenty-three years earlier, in the poem that stands here as oldest-song and that Farrokhi quotes in his introduction, where the three are similes for a love that goes on always. Here they are not comparisons but company — things he says he is intimate with. The last line can be read two ways, and Persian does not choose: آبی قدیمی‌ام is either "I am an old blue," which chimes with صمیمی‌ام ("I am intimate") three lines above, or "my old blue," the thing he is thirsting for a drop of.

Translator's Notes

Hossein Alizadeh (b. 1951) is a composer and a master of the tar and setar, and one of the central figures in what became of Persian classical music after the 1970s — which is what the whole dream-argument between the two men is about. عاشق (âsheq) is not the ghazal's "lover" here but the bardic minstrel of Khorasan, who sings to his own instrument. عثمان is Osman, the dotar master of Khaf in eastern Khorasan; فراغی is the maqâm named for the Turkmen poet Makhtumkuli Fraghi. The four "compositions" the dream-Alizadeh recites are invented inside the poem. جنگل نور is given as "the forest of light," though Nur is a forested county in Mazandaran and may be meant. Two things the Persian does that the English can only point at. شکسته means "broken" and is also the name of a gusheh in Persian classical music, so "your song is already more broken than anything they could break again" is also, exactly, "your song is already in Shekaste." And پنجه is one word for Alizadeh's playing fingers and for the small fingers of the carpet-weaving girls in the last composition — the poem's turn from the master's hand to the children's happens inside a single noun. "The cup was new, the wine old" is the argument in miniature; so is the poet's refusal to trade the blue of the old air for the lead-gray of the new. The line in quotation marks near the end of page 80 belongs to the "old poet" of two lines earlier, unidentified.

Translator's Notes

شب یلدا, Yalda, is the winter solstice: the longest night of the year, kept awake in company with poetry and pomegranates. A Yalda that is هجرانی — a Yalda of separation — is the longest night there is, spent apart, and the title says so before the poem starts. The last line makes a duet out of the two states the whole ghazal tradition runs on. وصل, union, and هجران, parting, are given a verb each: union strikes the strings (می‌زد) and parting sings (می‌خواند). One plays, the other sings, and the poet lies awake between them until morning.

Translator's Notes

زنده‌یاد means, word for word, *living memory*, and is what Persian calls someone who has died — the equivalent of 'the late'. The painter in this poem says twice that he would rather be glad than be زنده یاد, and the poem's last movement grants him the thing he refused. English has no honorific with a literal meaning that pleasant, so it is kept in quotation marks here. The opening two lines also rhyme on a pun that cannot cross: مهر is kindness or love, مُهر is a seal, and they are written with the same letters. The kindness of the kind man is what seals his mouth.

You said, lean on me — the maple makes the sky kinder. I said: you have no root in the soil... Leaves are meddling travellers... Cold and heat burn the leaves but not the roots... If only you would come back... from that far country... Tehran, spring 1384

Lantern

You said: lean on me, the maple makes the sky kinder. I said: you have no root in the soil. Leaves are meddling travellers. Cold and heat burn the leaves but they do not burn the roots. If only you would come back from that far country... Tehran, spring 1384

Translator's Notes

Fourteen years before this, answering a magazine that had asked him how the war decade treated him, Jafari wrote: *cold and heat burn the leaves and the branches, but not the roots.* He says it again here, almost word for word, to somebody who has left the country — the same sentence used first to explain how he survived and then to argue that leaving is a kind of not having survived.

Machine

Until you reach the rose-garden / the soul is not set free. All this transparency of yours / is not our portion. The tears of your angels / are the dandelion-clocks of release; while they still sit on the lash / no rapture gets to its feet. If only I could turn to fervour / turn to longing and to joy, like you and your angel / turn to light in a single night. Lantern

Until you reach the rose-garden, the soul is not set free. All this transparency of yours is not our portion. The tears of your angels are the dandelion-clocks of release; while they still sit on the lash, no rapture gets to its feet. If only I could turn to fervour, turn to longing and to joy, and like you and your angel turn to light in a single night. Though the bonds on us do not come open in one night, and this steep road is no road for a hand on a stick. All this freedom of yours is not our portion. Until you reach the rose-garden, the soul is not set free. Winter 1385 Iraj Zand's last exhibition at Golestan Gallery / Transparent Angels

Translator's Notes

Iraj Zand died in ۱۳۸۶. The poem is dated from his last show, which was called *Transparent Angels*, and it is made almost entirely out of that title: the transparency, the angels, and their tears are the paintings on the wall. گلستان is a rose-garden and also the name of the gallery the show hung in, so the line *until you reach the rose-garden the soul is not set free* names the room the poem was written in and the paradise it is asking about, in one word. On the printed page the poem is set in two columns — the Persian way of laying out rhymed couplets, first half-line on the right, second on the left. It reads across, not down.
